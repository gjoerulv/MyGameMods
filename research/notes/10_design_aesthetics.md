## 1. Vladimir Kuzmanov, "On the Aesthetics of the Adventure Map" (2006)

Framing: the article is about single-player and RPG maps, "where the settings are as
important as the balance or the story." Kuzmanov notes that gameplay threads abound
but "few threads deal with the looks of the adventure map."

Context on HoMM1/2: he observes that in the early games all adventure objects were
symbols — "they used to represent things, not show their actual size." Some of "the
best maps EVER" were made with the Heroes II editor, but they were maps, not worlds;
the transition to believable landscapes began with H3. (For a HoMM2 mapmaker this is a
license: HoMM2 aesthetics are symbolic/iconic, so composition and readability matter
more than literal realism.)

Core principle stated up front: immersion. Heroes was loved as "a fantasy world
swarming with fantastic creatures and dazzling spells" — "A good mapmaker doesn't
underestimate the importance of immersion."

### 1) Plan your map (before decorating)
- Think about the map before opening the editor. Lay out towns and other key features
  first; "draw a mini-map on a piece of paper if you must."
- Choose the size to fit the story/theme/idea, not for popularity. XL maps get the
  most downloads, "but need your map be so large?" Oversizing risks spoiling gameplay
  AND leaves vast areas that must be filled. He cites Kristo: "there are few things
  worse than having a large section of a map with nothing in it."
- Deliberate emptiness is allowed as an exception if it serves the theme (his example:
  a desert "left barren for purpose").
- You can judge a map's quality by opening it in the editor first. "A good mapmaker
  devotes as much time to planning, storytelling and testing" as to decorating.
- "Do not use brushes and pre-done random maps. They are ugly as hell." (i.e. never
  ship auto-generated terrain as-is.)

### 2) Starting area
- Hook the player immediately. He quotes Shae_Trielle's "Dr. Dre logic": people judge
  a song in the first fifteen seconds; a map is judged the same way.
- "A town, a sawmill and an ore mine are fine," but the opening must contain
  something that glues the player to the screen — a striking visual, an unusual
  situation, an immediate mini-goal.
- The starting area must also fit the story (theme-consistent terrain, objects,
  message text from turn 1).

### 3) Unique concepts
- Players "have seen it all by now" — design key areas and important-object
  placements in an original way (a labyrinth, a field pattern, a built-up "town" made
  of objects). Repeating patterns / multiple copies of one object deliberately is a
  legitimate compositional device.
- But respect editor limitations; don't force concepts the tileset can't sell.
- "Do not put objects where they do not belong!" Environmental logic rules: you need
  "a really good story to back up snow hills on lava." His running gag: why are
  Orcs guarding a Faerie Ring? A random guard on a themed object breaks fiction —
  match guard species/faction to the object and region where possible.

### 4) Fear of the empty space (density balance)
- The inverse error of emptiness: cramming. "Move the objects away from each other."
  Don't cluster objects tightly unless it is a deliberate composition.
- Leave room for heroes to move freely — playability of the decoration matters.
- Both extremes (bare and overstuffed) break immersion.

### 5) Polishing phase
- Structure pass first (mountain ranges etc.), then a long polishing pass; polishing
  is "as tedious, but more rewarding."
- Use objects across their nominal terrain categories when they visually fit (his H3
  examples: dead vegetation from "rough" used underground, subterranean mushrooms in
  swamp, moss logs on grass, dirt hills on rough, a small sand bush that fits most
  terrains and can block key squares). HoMM2 equivalent: raid every terrain tab of the
  editor for cross-terrain decoration, and use small obstacles to control pathing.
- Perfectionism is normal: spending "ten minutes... moving one single rock" is part
  of the craft.
- The believability test: "could my map really exist?" The magic is lost the moment
  players are reminded they're playing a computer game.
- Payoff asymmetry: "few would notice the results of your hard work," but everybody
  would notice its absence.

### 6) Easter eggs
- Hide pleasant surprises and tie them into the story; they create memorable moments.

### 7) Testing the in-game look
- While balance-testing, "pay attention to details on the adventure map" — scenery
  "might look a bit different" in-game than in the editor (lighting, animation,
  overlap, fog of war). Always verify the map at game resolution, not just in the
  editor.
- Use testers' impressions but keep authorial judgment, "unless the feedback is
  extremely negative."

### H4-section principles that transfer to HoMM2
- Layered decoration workflow: terrain → main objects → frame areas with trees at
  edges/corners → ground detail ("more near the woods and less around the roads") →
  final touches (in H2: small critter/animation objects). Decoration density gradients
  look natural; uniform sprinkle looks generated.
- Roads: "connect the objects with roads; the scenery looks more natural," plus
  players appreciate the movement bonus. Roads can also act as area texture, and
  terrain strips (dirt path across grass) can fake trails.
- Make vital spots stand out with special terrain patches and structures around them
  (landmark composition — frame the important thing).
- "Wow" moments (term from reviewer Psychobabble) are essential: one claustrophobic
  forest passage, one big waterfall, one surprising vista per map is worth more than
  uniform prettiness.
- Repurpose objects to depict things the editor doesn't have (his examples: ghosts
  placed in dead trees + blacksmith hut = demolished haunted mansion; gold mines +
  cursed ground + geysers = oil fields). Object combinations tell stories without text.
- Picture-maps / minimap art: Ururam Tururam "sacrificed the adventure map in order
  to get a dazzling mini-map" — a warning that minimap art and playable terrain
  trade off; the minimap is a real aesthetic surface players see constantly.
- Things placed for atmosphere alone are legitimate: statues and runes "create the
  atmosphere of your map. Things have a way of telling their own stories."
- Homage is fine, plagiarism is not: recreate scenes from other games/books but
  credit them — "it is not plagiarism if you admit it."

## 2. Kristo's School of Quality Mapmaking (written for Heroes II, adapted to H3)

Thesis: "Mapmaking... is as much art as it is designing a challenging and fun
scenario." The stock maps look "as though the land could actually exist" — "This is
the biggest key to a high quality map" (exception: intentional "maps of
impossibility," where "the weirder it looks, the better").

Step One — Draw the landmass first.
- "Anything square looks too 'man-made'"; coastlines and terrain borders need jagged,
  irregular edges. "The more random it looks... the more natural."
- Apply the same care to terrain-type boundaries, not just coastlines.
- Don't skimp on secondary areas (his example: gorgeous surface + underground made of
  "a couple of big squares"). HoMM2 analog: treat late-game islands/back areas with
  the same care as the start.
- Legitimate shortcut: let a random generator rough out a landmass, erase all
  objects, then hand-edit the land — generation as clay, never as product.

Step Two — Place towns next.
- Place most towns (owned and neutral) before decorating; they anchor everything.
- "Fit the towns to the land, not vice versa." Match town type to native/friendly
  terrain; every player needs a viable start (no one stuck in harsh terrain).
- Towns nested in a pocket of trees or mountains look better — pure aesthetics but
  cheap to do.

Step Three — Mountains and trees (the long boring step).
- Break up ALL wide-open spaces now. "There are few things worse" than a big empty
  section.
- Vary obstacle sprites: "pick them at random. Under no circumstances should you
  repeat the same object over and over." "Monotone forests and mountain ranges look
  really ugly." (Directly transferable: HoMM2 has multiple tree/mountain/rock
  variants per terrain — rotate them.)
- Think ahead to mine placement while roughing terrain: a Lumber Mill amid groves
  looks right; awkward mines (his: Sulfur Dunes) can be swapped for alternatives that
  blend (his trick: Abandoned Mine set to sulfur — "bonus: it's inherently guarded").
- Done-criterion: foliage "starts to look real" and no wide-open spaces remain.

Step Four — Mines and defenses (guard scaling doctrine).
- Mines go in "logical pockets" of the terrain; if none exist, sculpt some.
- "Every town needs unrestricted access to wood and ore" — unguarded sawmill + ore
  mine per player, reachable within ~1 turn, or the map dies on hard difficulties.
- Rare-resource and gold mines can be guarded; use "a stepladder approach" — guard
  strength scales with the mine's importance to the nearest player.
- Contextual scaling: a mercury lab near a Castle-type player is low value → guard
  lightly. Gold mines are always high value → always adequately defended.
- Distance is part of the economy: basic resource buildings near home; rare producers
  anywhere — "part of the challenge... is being short on certain resources."

Step Five — The other stuff (dwellings, teleporters, pickups).
- Fill medium spaces with interactive objects; shrink remaining gaps but "don't
  overfill the map."
- Guard things in proportion to their importance (same stepladder rule).
- Anti-pattern: "the tendency to defend locations that are already defended" (H3
  example: monsters stacked in front of a Dragon Utopia). HoMM2 analog: don't put a
  guard stack in front of a Dragon City or Daemon Cave — the object already fights.
- Use wandering monsters to section off each player's starting region; where regions
  aren't clean, place monsters so players are "slowed down by being forced to fight"
  — pacing via guard placement, not walls alone.

Step Six — Finishing touches.
- Flowers, rocks, small natural features; loose resource piles in and around start
  areas; "be sure not to overdo it."
- Only now write map description/properties, because you finally know how it plays.
- Then playtest with reveal-map cheats to audit the whole surface.

## 3. Ururam Tururam, "Mapmaking Methodology" (game-agnostic extracts)

Pre-production:
- Know your purpose (private fun vs. publication); publication demands quality — his
  estimate: "about 70% of user made scenarios... should never be released."
- Required skills checklist: spatial imagination, aesthetic judgment, storytelling,
  program-like thinking about gameplay flow, stamina ("if you feel your map is 90%
  ready you are just in the middle"), willingness to learn from opening others' maps
  in the editor, working alone, and objectivity about your own work.
- Keep a separate scratch/test map for experiments; never experiment on the real map.

Map idea and size:
- Start from one of three seeds: a landscape vision, a world/story, or a unique
  win-mechanic. "At all costs avoid making another 'go and kill everything' map."
- Size guidance: "If you are not sure, choose M or L. Sizes S and XL should be
  reserved for special purposes" — "XL-sized maps tend to be boring while S-sized
  ones are usually too small." (HoMM2 sizes are S36/M72/L108/XL144 — same logic.)
- Decide up front: water bodies (lakes/rivers/seas/archipelagos), number of regions,
  and the region-to-player ratio.

Layout archetypes (his taxonomy, easiest → hardest):
1. Uncontrolled chaotic — random stuff at random places. "Easy but dirty. Don't."
2. Linear — one path; only works if the storyline carries it.
3. Region-based — countries with sharp borders (mountains/forest/rivers), passages
   guarded by garrisons/strong neutrals; each region often has its own terrain type
   and an internal guard-strength band (stronger stacks only on treasures/keys).
4. Open — free exploration where "the shortest way is the most dangerous one";
   several routes with different guard costs and lengths. Fails into linear (if the
   best route is obvious) or chaos (if no pattern reads).
5. Controlled chaotic — deliberate surrealism; must be story-backed and is very hard.
6. Domain-based — regions with sub-designs; L/XL only; "For experts only."
- "an hour of designing saves at least 3 hours of fixing!"

Build order and pacing:
- Shape land + place cities first; run the map once for every color immediately to
  catch "too straight lines... different terrain connections" early.
- Back up constantly, with version numbers.
- Implement the playable skeleton (obstacles, quests, key mines, dwellings) BEFORE
  any treasure/monster sugar; "work in edit-test cycles," testing every path as added.
- Predictability warning: on 90% of maps an ore mine or sawmill telegraphs "castle
  nearby!" — and a road does the same. Use or subvert the tell knowingly, but early
  wood/ore access must still exist.
- Sugar phase: decorations, artifacts, treasures — then "test, test, test!" because
  every decoration "can block a critical way." Don't stop with some areas polished
  and others bald: "Maps with some areas finished and some other bald look peculiar."
- Decoration balance: "similar objects close to each other (looks boring)" vs
  "different objects close to each other (looks inconsistent)" — walking that line
  is the whole job and "usually takes a lot of time."
- Roads speed play and "look nice when made properly."
- Aids for AI players (extra resources, pre-built structures, flagged mines) are a
  legitimate balancing tool; AI plays poorly.
- Custom names + 2–3 sentence biographies for placed heroes/towns are "a sign of
  quality." Sign your map once (roadsign or endgame message), not more.
- Terrain aesthetics motivation: "Nice look encourages players to explore, while
  ugly one discourages."
- Trees hiding passable tiles: either block hidden gaps with invisible-ish rocks,
  build "solid" forests with a few narrow true paths (verify passability!), or hide
  artifacts/resources behind foliage as rewards for observant players.

Betatest and release:
- Self-play every color first; 2–5 testers, at least one expert and one non-expert.
- Ship at the "good enough point"; perfection never arrives. Don't publish v1.1s.
- Map name + in-editor description must carry the pitch; "your maps will often be
  distributed without" companion text files.
- Difficulty labeling: avoid "easy" and avoid boastful "champion"; label honestly.

## 4. Charles Watkins, "Behind the Curtain: Mapmaker as Entertainer"

Frame: "Essentially, the mapmaker is an entertainer." Every map type is judged on
whether players had a good time. Three pleasure sources structure the series:
DISCOVERY (anticipation + surprise), SUCCESS (a ladder of intermediate goals up to a
satisfying finale, balanced between too easy and too hard), AESTHETICS.

Part 3 (Aesthetics) principles:
- Visual reveals: stage graphical surprises — a vista around a corner, a
  view-object unveiling a hidden composition. In HoMM2 the analogs are Observation
  Towers / Magellan's Maps / Eye artifacts and simply path routing that controls
  when a composition enters view.
- Use the map itself as signage: "Illuminated objectives stand out on the world map"
  — since players skip text, visually highlighted key locations are the most
  reliable way to direct them (landmark = navigation).
- The mapmaker must reason about what the player can SEE from each vantage
  (scouting radii, fog of war) — plan sightlines; it takes trial and error.
- Against gratuitous decoration: "gratuitous graphics can become a distraction —
  flowers in the snow" prompt the wrong questions. Anomalies are fine only if
  rationalized by the fiction.
- Internal consistency doctrine: fantasy worlds "must be internally consistent or
  else the magic can be replaced with confusion."
- Player-expectation doctrine: "If the mapmaker departs too far from these
  expectations, players feel lost and disappointed"; balance surprise against
  predictability (from his failed experimental map where fighting was optional).
- Storytelling and trimmings are art surfaces; note HoMM2 already hosted "lighter
  weight" literary maps (e.g. his own H2 "Lord of the Rings").

## 6. Heroes Community "Mapmaking gurus" thread (2013, p.1) — transferable nuggets

- Realistic power progression beats stunts (Salamandre): players resent being given a
  huge army then having it confiscated; power curves should feel earned.
- Save-scumming is a design constraint: any "lose exactly N troops" gate will be
  gamed by reloading.
- Strong-defender pattern: a powerful enemy hero on a short patrol leash (H3) guards
  an AI town without ever invading — early-game "insurmountable neighbor" pressure
  with a later payoff. HoMM2 analog: strong garrisoned AI castle or well-placed
  neutral stacks; note H2's AI famously "spread the wealth around" among heroes
  rather than making one super-hero.
- Choke-point events/guards at border passes pace the game; telegraph invisible
  dangers through rumors/signs so surprises feel fair.
- Corribus: "Weave a good story out of it, and players will barely notice they're
  being manipulated" — scripting hacks are fine if narratively dressed.

  hmm2gold maps sorted into folders.
- NO per-map metadata: no sizes, types, ratings, or download counts visible;
  "detailed description of files... are inside of archives." So homm2.com answers
  "what exists" (a canon of ~1000+ community maps, dominated by PoL-format) but not
  "what is well-regarded."
- maps4heroes.com/heroes2/maps.php is the visible-ratings venue but tiny: 11 HoMM2
  maps total (all Price of Loyalty), sizes spread S→XL. Downloads: Omnicresta
  (L, 6 players, 1,176 DLs), timeVSgravity (XL, Hard, 6p, 1,108), Long live heroes
  (XL, Expert, 4p, 1,059); most maps have 0–1 ratings. Conclusion: multiplayer L/XL
  maps draw the downloads even in this niche, echoing Kuzmanov's "XL scenarios get
  the most downloads" — and his warning still applies.
- Community mapmaking energy has shifted to fheroes2 (open-source HoMM2 engine),
  whose in-game editor adds passability display and an Auto Playtest feature —
  useful for the "test the in-game look" and pathing principles above.
- Named well-regarded H2 mapmakers encountered in sources: Timothy Duncan/Corribus
  (praised at rpgcodex; his H2 "The Cabal"), Charles Watkins (H2 "Lord of the
  Rings"), plus NWC stock maps (e.g. "Broken Alliance") as the look-and-feel canon.

## 8. Practical checklist: making a HoMM2 map look handcrafted, not generated

Synthesized from all sources above; HoMM2-specific framing is mine.

Macro / planning
- [ ] Concept, story, and win condition decided before the editor opens; minimap
      sketched on paper; towns and key sites placed first.
- [ ] Size fits content (default M/L; S and XL only with a reason). No region exists
      that you don't have content or a theme for.
- [ ] Pick a layout archetype consciously (linear / region / open / domain);
      shortest routes are the most dangerous; at least one meaningful route choice.
- [ ] Coastlines and terrain borders jagged and irregular — no straight edges, no
      rectangles, no single-terrain continents. Terrain patches vary in size.

Starting area (the first 15 seconds)
- [ ] Start = town + reachable unguarded wood/ore within ~1 turn + one hook (a
      striking visual, an odd object, an immediate question).
- [ ] Starting terrain matches the player's faction and the story.
- [ ] Loose resource piles seeded around start areas, lightly.

Objects, guards, pacing
- [ ] Every object sits in an environmentally logical spot (sawmill among trees,
      mines against rock, shrines framed by scenery). No "snow hills on lava."
- [ ] Guards match theme and value: stepladder scaling by importance (gold mine >
      rare mine > convenience); no guards on self-defending objects; guard species
      fit the region (no Orcs at the Faerie Ring).
- [ ] Wandering/blocking stacks partition the map and set the pace; borders read as
      geography (mountains, rivers, forests), passes are visible choke points.
- [ ] Treasure value scales with guard cost and distance from start; nothing
      valuable is free, nothing worthless is expensive.
- [ ] Mines/roads as "castle nearby" tells are used or subverted deliberately.

Decoration (the polishing pass)
- [ ] No large empty spaces anywhere — and no crammed corridors either; heroes can
      move freely through decorated areas.
- [ ] Obstacle sprites rotated constantly; never the same tree/mountain twice in a
      row; monotone ranges are the #1 generated-look symptom.
- [ ] Decoration in layers and gradients: frame with big objects, fill with medium,
      dust with small; denser near forests/landmarks, sparser near roads and paths.
- [ ] Cross-terrain object borrowing where sprites fit; small obstacles used to
      shape pathing and block exploits.
- [ ] Every region polished to the same standard — no finished front yard with a
      bald back yard.
- [ ] One or two "wow" landmarks: a framed vital spot, an object-combination scene
      (ruin, farmstead, battlefield), a staged reveal on the approach route.
- [ ] A few atmosphere-only objects and an Easter egg tied to the story.

Readability / minimap
- [ ] Minimap looks composed: distinct terrain masses, readable regions, visible
      routes; it's the surface players stare at all game. Don't sacrifice the
      playable map for minimap art (or do it knowingly).
- [ ] Key objectives are visually prominent on the adventure map — landmark
      composition as signage, since players skip text.
- [ ] Sightlines checked: what does the player see when each composition first
      enters view?

Consistency & testing
- [ ] Internal consistency audit: "could my map really exist?" Every anomaly is
      rationalized by the fiction.
- [ ] In-game look pass at play resolution (editor ≠ game: animation, palette, fog);
      walk every path; verify passability behind trees/rocks.
- [ ] Full playthrough as every color; 2–5 testers incl. one expert and one novice;
      honest difficulty label; name + description written last, carried in the map
      file itself.
- [ ] Custom names/dialog for key heroes and towns; one signature, no more.

Generated-vs-handcrafted tells (quick diagnostic)
Generated look: uniform object sprinkle, repeated identical sprites, square terrain
patches, unguarded riches next to overguarded trash, objects floating context-free,
no roads, no landmarks, uniform density, mute minimap.
Handcrafted look: density gradients, varied sprites, jagged organic borders, guards
that tell you where you are on the power curve, object clusters that depict scenes,
roads linking civilization, one memorable set piece per region, a minimap you could
recognize at a glance.
